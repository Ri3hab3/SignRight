About the Bill of Sale for Personal Property

A bill of sale is a legal document that records the private sale of personal property — furniture, electronics, tools, jewelry, livestock, or any other item. It creates written proof that ownership has transferred from seller to buyer on a specific date, at a specific price, and in a described condition. This simple document protects both parties if questions arise later about ownership, condition, or payment.

When you need a bill of sale

  • High-value items — jewelry, artwork, antiques, musical instruments, or anything over $200 where proof of price matters
  • Tax deductible items — home office equipment, business tools, or assets you may depreciate
  • Insurance claims — proof of original purchase price for high-value personal property
  • Online marketplace sales — eBay, Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist (protects seller from fraud claims after delivery)
  • Estate sales — documenting the transfer of inherited personal property between family members or to third parties
  • Business-to-business transfers — equipment, furniture, or inventory sold between companies

Bill of sale vs. receipt — what's the difference?

A receipt confirms payment was received. A bill of sale goes further: it confirms the transfer of ownership, describes the item in detail, sets out the condition of sale (as-is or with warranty), and is signed by both parties. For anything of real value, a bill of sale is the correct document because it is enforceable as a contract in most states.

How to fill out a bill of sale step by step

Use this form to fill out each section — the live preview updates as you type. Here is what each section requires:

  • Item description: Be specific. Include model, make, color, approximate dimensions, and any identifying marks or damage. Vague descriptions lead to disputes.
  • Serial numbers: Include for electronics, appliances, tools, or any item with a traceable identifier. This is critical for insurance claims.
  • Seller and buyer details: Full legal name and current address for both parties. Phone and email are optional but recommended.
  • Sale price and date: State the exact cash amount agreed upon. The date of sale determines when ownership legally transferred.
  • As-Is clause: Select this for most private sales. It means the buyer inspected the item and accepts it in its current condition, and the seller has no further liability.
  • Governing state: Select the state where the transaction takes place. This determines which law applies if there is a future dispute.
  • Signatures: Both parties must sign. Print two copies — one for the buyer, one for the seller. Keep yours.

State-specific requirements

For general personal property, no US state requires a specific form — this generic bill of sale is valid in all 50 states. However, a few categories have additional requirements. Firearms transfers must comply with state and federal law, which may require a licensed dealer. Vehicles, boats, and trailers require dedicated title documents and DMV filings in most states — use our Vehicle Bill of Sale for those. For high-value transactions over $500, having signatures notarized strengthens the document considerably.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a bill of sale for small items?

Legally, no. But it's smart for anything over $100 or anything that might have tax or insurance implications. Takes 3 minutes to fill out, provides years of legal protection.

Does this work for selling a boat, RV, or vehicle?

Use our dedicated Vehicle Bill of Sale for those — it includes VIN, odometer disclosure, and other specific fields DMVs require.

What about selling a firearm?

Firearms require a separate bill of sale with specific disclosures that vary by state. Some states require transfers go through a licensed dealer. Don't use this generic form for firearms — consult your state law.

Can I use this for business inventory?

Yes for simple business-to-business transfers. For ongoing commercial supply agreements, use a proper Service Agreement or Supply Contract instead.

Should I get a witness or notary?

Not required for most transactions. For items over $500 or particularly sensitive sales (family heirlooms, estate items), a notary adds legal weight. Costs $5–$25 at most banks or UPS stores.
